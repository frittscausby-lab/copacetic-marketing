Why Conference Content Needs a Human in the Loop

Fritts Causby  ·  Copacetic Marketing  ·  2 min read

Transcription software has improved such that it is tempting to treat a conference as a solved problem. Why review the content or hire a writer if it isn’t necessary?

Simply recording the sessions and running the audio through a tool can result in a finished product that appears to be acceptable for a busy professional, at least on a quick review. The sequence can feel efficient; however, it poses a significant risk to the credibility of the firm.

One problem is that transcription software often stumbles on what matters most in professional settings, which is proper names, case names, statutes, acronyms and numbers. A Southern drawl, a Boston vowel or a speaker working in a second language will frequently confuse the technology.

Precise terms may be transformed into different words that sound plausible, which may not appear to be a significant issue at first glance. A small error can become especially problematic because it is not uncommon for AI to present a guess with confidence. A speaker's name can appear three ways in one document. A figure can be off by a digit. A reader who knows the field will catch the error and trust the author a little less.

Transcripts and the summaries produced by AI often lose context. Speakers joke, hedge, qualify and correct themselves. A person in the room hears the pause before an answer and the moment a panelist walks back a claim. A transcript flattens all of it, and someone has to decide what the speaker meant before it is published under the name of the firm.

Similarly, technology will gloss over some of the details and anecdotes a human reader may find interesting. The finished product assembled by the AI may include an exhaustive summary, but it will often miss some of the points and insights that made the conference worth having.

Selection is a key point in favor of hiring a writer. Choosing ideas that could resonate and deleting other information is an editorial judgment about an audience. This is more complex than a summarizing task and significantly, it’s essential for developing content that is engaging and easy to read.

Accountability is another factor that is worthy of consideration. A quote from a speaker or an interpretation of a regulation on the company’s letterhead creates obligations. Some remarks are made off the record or in a setting where the speaker would not expect to be quoted, and software has no way to tell the difference.

Fabrications and hallucinations bring legal risks. Tools that are tasked with building content from a transcript are focused on pleasing the user. The result is that AI will occasionally fill gaps with plausible text that was never said, and a fluent error is harder to catch than a rough one.

These are a few of the reasons why my conference work begins in the room. I attend the sessions in person and record them, but I also listen carefully and take notes to ensure the accuracy of the finished product. Every name, figure and quote is checked against the recording before drafting, and the client reviews the draft before anything is published. Software speeds up the transcript, and a person takes responsibility for the article.

The method was developed for a regional law firm and applies to any field where the people in the room know things worth publishing, including real estate, finance, construction, healthcare, energy and professional associations.

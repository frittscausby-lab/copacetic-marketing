Sweet Summer Bliss: Mouth-Watering Grilled Pineapple Recipes

For those who are fortunate enough to have visited the state of Hawaii and more specifically, the island of Oahu, it’s likely the journey is tied to the sweet, tantalizing aroma of fresh pineapple wafting through the fields at Dole Plantation. This is partially because smell and memory are closely intertwined, due to the structure of the brain, and the fact the plantation is smack dab in between Honolulu and the North Shore.

With fields of pineapple stretching as far as the eye can see and lush, green mountains as a backdrop, it’s worth pulling the car over for lunch, a stroll through the garden maze or a tour of the botanical gardens. Fresher really is better and a visit is not complete without a sample of the pineapple float, the chocolate-covered, coconut-sprinkled pineapples or the Kalua BBQ Pork sandwich, which has island-style smoked pulled pork covered with pineapple on a sweet roll.

Pineapples are as beautiful as they are tasty and, incidentally, the origin of the name stems from their pine cone-like appearance. If pine cones were as delicious and versatile as the pineapple, hunger could potentially be a thing of the past here in the South.

Another worthy aside is the idea that there are a number of similarities between the Islands and the Southeast, fresh fish, a penchant for BBQ and an ocean-oriented culture being chief among them. And what could be a better pairing for blackened Mahi or Grouper than grilled pineapple?

Sheri Castle, host of The Key Ingredient on PBS, has some mouth-watering ideas. “A grilled pineapple can be a sweet counterpoint to something savory, such as with a grilled pineapple salsa, a garnish for a black bean soup, or inside a baked sweet potato,” said Castle.

A food writer and professional cook, Castle is frequently asked for tips on how to prepare her grilled pineapple and raw rhubarb salsa, which she uses as a topping for fish tacos. “For dessert, I like to pair it with ice cream and make it the bottom of a sundae. And you can go tropical by using pineapple sorbet or coconut sorbet. I love coconut sorbet with pineapple and some fresh lime or things like that,” Castle explained.

For a more traditional approach, Castle’s advice is to pair grilled pineapple with vanilla ice cream, caramel sauce and some sort of crunchy nuts. “Because the pineapple is always tender even before you apply heat, and it doesn’t go soggy, it will give you a textural element in that otherwise very creamy and soft sundae,” added Castle.

The good news is that, in terms of preparation, pineapple is one of the easiest fruits to grill. “A minute or two on each side is enough,” says Castle, “because it’s so high in sugar. It really likes those last smoldering embers, so it’s the perfect end for a cookout. You can be eating while the embers die down and then hit that pineapple when it’s time for dessert without having to fire up the grill again.”

Slicing it for direct placement on the grill or dicing it to place on a grill pan is enough, but Castle has an idea that ups the flavor profile significantly. “It’s really easy because you don’t have to wrap it in foil or use a grill grate. You don’t have to do any of that. But one thing you can do is to dip it in melted butter and sugar. That way you can get a true caramelization crunch on the outside,” noted Castle.

This process is something that Castle refers to as gilding the lily, essentially the act of adding unnecessary ornamentation to something that is already beautiful. “It implies that it’s better, but on the edge of being way too much,” she said. “The butter isn’t to help the pineapple, it’s to help the sugar adhere…and it turns crispy, like little bits of caramel.”

The Surf and Turf Chimichurri recipe provided by Bill Scott, managing partner of Cape Fear Seafood Company, also incorporates grilled pineapple. It includes grilled shrimp and either filet or ribeye served with Chimichurri, which Scott describes as a simple pesto-style sauce.

To begin, Scott recommends pre-making the Chimichurri, as it is best if it sits for a few hours after prepping. The following ingredients are used to prepare the Chimichurri:

  • 1 bunch cilantro
  • 1 bunch parsley
  • 2 or 3 garlic cloves peeled and minced
  • 1 shallot bulb minced (optional)
  • 1 tsp black pepper
  • 1/2 tsp or more crushed red chili flake (optional but a little heat is nice with the sweet)
  • 1 tsp salt (more or less to taste)
  • 1/4 cup red wine vinegar (other vinegar works but red wine is the best for this sauce)
  • 1 cup extra virgin olive oil (any oil will work but olive oil produces the best flavor)

The first step is to loose chop the parsley and cilantro and place it in a mixing bowl. From there, add the other ingredients, except for the olive oil, and stir to blend them together. The final step involves whisking in the olive oil while stirring, then refrigerating it until it’s time to serve. “This pairs well with both steak and seafood, so it’s the perfect accompaniment,” said Scott.

For the pineapple, Scott recommends grilling them first and then setting them aside until the steak and shrimp is ready. The process begins with cutting a pineapple in half, and then cutting planks from it that are approximately one half inch in thickness.

“You want them to be slightly thick or they will wilt when grilling,” advises Scott. The grill should be covered with a pan spray before it’s hot. After the grill is heated, add the planks and rotate a half turn to get a diamond char pattern.

“There is no need to cook both sides, you don’t want to overcook the pineapple, just get good char marks for flavor,” noted Scott.

Use medium to large peeled and deveined shrimp and skewer separately from the filet and/or ribeye, since the shrimp will cook faster. The beef should be cut into approximately one inch chunks and either skewered or placed directly on the grill. The steak should be seasoned with blackening spice if it’s available, but salt and pepper will work just as well.

“This is a quick recipe to build but it will taste like you spent some time on it,” added Scott, whose restaurant donates $1 to the Trawick Pediatric Cardiology Research Fund at Duke University every time the dish is sold.

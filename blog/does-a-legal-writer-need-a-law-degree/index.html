Does a Legal Writer Need a Law Degree?

Fritts Causby  ·  Copacetic Marketing  ·  2 min read

A job post for a legal writer often mentions a JD as a requirement. The reasoning is certainly understandable, as the law is precise and full of unapproachable jargon. The stakes for clients are real, of course, and a careless word can expose the firm to a variety of legal risks.

The requirement combines two different jobs, however. And significantly, the firms that insist on having a writer with a legal degree often pay for a knowledge base that is not required for the role.

Knowing the law is the job of the attorney. Writing is foundational to earning a legal degree and the daily responsibilities of many lawyers, who are often proficient explaining complex, nuanced ideas and crafting dense, careful prose that works in a legal brief.

Making the content engaging and approachable for different audiences is the job of the writer. In an effective thought leadership article, the substance comes from the attorney. The writer adds transitions, conversational elements and research where it is necessary.

The substance originates from the ideas and analysis of the attorney. This can be gathered through interviews, seminar recordings, emails or notes. The attorney reviews each draft before publication. That review is the real safeguard, and no writer's credentials can replace it.

The reason, of course, is that many issues require legal judgment. Some cases and decisions are extremely complicated and can be confusing, even for insiders. Similarly, it can be difficult to determine whether an article drifts from information into advice.

The firm and its compliance department may want to review the article to ensure it complies with state bar requirements pertaining to attorney advertising. A writer with experience in the legal field will know when to ask questions and when outside research may be required.

A writer that is new to the legal field will require more time and effort from the attorney and the firm. Writers with experience in the legal arena typically have a working knowledge of the subject matter and an understanding of how to explain complex ideas in a manner that is approachable for the average person.

I have been a ghostwriter for a regional law firm since 2021 and the process has remained the same: the attorney supplies the substance, I shape it and the attorney signs off before anything is published. The arrangement has relied on this review process instead of a degree and of course, nothing on this page is legal advice.

How to Repurpose Content from Seminars and Conferences

Fritts Causby  ·  Copacetic Marketing  ·  2 min read

Hosting a client seminar or sending key people to a conference is expensive. The content that is produced by the subject matter experts at these events has already been paid for, so it is worth considering publishing it to foster engagement with existing clients, prospects and stakeholders.

In many cases, however, the content evaporates when the event is over. The raw material could be used on a variety of platforms, so it is easy to see the value in repurposing it.

Afterward, the raw material supports more formats than most firms expect. A single session could produce an in-depth article or client alert with timely updates and conversational insights from the speakers that are not available in other outlets.

Long-form content can be easily broken down into newsletter items, a series of LinkedIn posts or even a separate landing page for the business.

The raw material could be used to create a frequently asked questions page or an internal memo that captures the competitive intelligence the public pieces leave out.

Using the content to develop a pitch to a trade publication or local business journal is another high-value, low-effort idea that would represent a home run for the business if the article was published.

Repurposing the material requires some planning before the event, however, as a decision will need to be made about how to capture it effectively. Content from a seminar hosted by the company belongs to the business and can be freely used, subject to the speakers' approval.

A conference session delivered by someone else can be reported on and quoted with attribution. A best practice is to identify the sessions that may be interesting to clients in advance. The next step is to reach out to the speakers to ask permission.

During the event, record with permission and take notes anyway. The recording preserves the exact wording. The notes preserve the sequence of the speakers, questions from the audience and a variety of additional details that may not be easily understood by transcription software.

Quotes and figures should be reviewed against the recording. Similarly, any individual who is named in the article should have a chance to review the draft.

The timing is important because anything with recent updates could be viewed as news. The same article that is published after two months loses that advantage, as a competitor may have already published on the topic. The initial articles should be drafted while the session is fresh, and the speakers are still reachable for edits and approvals.

I developed this process for a regional law firm, attending seminars in person and turning the sessions into published articles. The same sequence applies to a real estate convention, a financial industry conference or an association's annual meeting, and a conversation before the next event is the easiest time to plan for it.

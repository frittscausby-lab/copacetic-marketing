Culinary Collard Creations: Two Southern Chefs Share their Secrets

Nothing spells Southern more than collard greens. For those who were lucky enough to be raised in the Southeast or visit on a consistent basis, the aroma of collard greens wafting in from the kitchen is almost certain to conjure fond memories of grandma’s kitchen and/or potlucks with family and friends.

For outsiders, it may seem a bit counterintuitive to cook vegetables in a stock of bacon grease or butter. As the saying goes, however, we have a different way of doing things here in the South and, simply put, the results of the flavorful recipes listed here don’t lie.

The preparation of collard greens is iconic to the South, similar to Calabash-style seafood, grits, oyster roasts and barbecue. One may wonder, however, why the flavor profile seems to vary so much from house to house and restaurant to restaurant. The simple explanation is that there is no right or wrong answer; the proof is in the pudding and, without getting too far off track, part of the appeal with cooking is that, in some ways, it’s more of an art than an exact science.

This is all a long way of saying the magazine has received a significant amount of demand for collard green recipes, and the ones listed here do not have exact measurements. Instead, these recipes are the result of decades of experience in the kitchen, using a pinch of this and a pinch of that, finding what works and what doesn’t. If nothing else, this can be an invitation to experiment and infuse a tidge of conservative creativity into the kitchen.

You asked, we listened and, drawing from the editor/owner’s extensive local network of friends and colleagues, we found two established Southern ladies with differing viewpoints, who were willing to share the recipes that have kept their families coming back for more. Both are known as amazing Southern cooks; both agree that stalking the collards is a critical first step. Those from other areas may call this removing the ribs, regardless; this includes running a sharp knife along the stalk or rib to separate the leaf.

After stalking approximately seven leaves, Miss Freida McDonald recommends folding the leaves in half, then rolling them similar to a pinwheel sandwich, sushi roll or cigar. This aids in slicing the leaves into the thinnest possible portions.

The sliced leaves should be washed thoroughly, and then placed in a colander to drain. While the leaves are drying, the next step is to find a large pot and start preparing the stock. Fill the bottom of the pot with just enough water to prevent the greens from scorching.

“All greens are basically water so you don’t want to use a lot of water,” explained Miss Freida. “I normally season my greens with smoked turkey necks or turkey wings, but today I’m using Amish Roll Butter.”

As the name implies, the butter, which is available at Food Lion and Publix, comes in a roll. Miss Freida slices it in a portion that is equivalent to approximately a stick and a half. The next step is to include a liberal shake or two of both granulated garlic and granulated onion. Anne’s Old Fashioned Chicken Base is the next essential ingredient and Miss Freida advocates for including a large serving spoon-size amount.

“Do not add salt because the chicken base has plenty of salt in it,” cautioned Miss Freida, who has always cooked in large portions because she has four children and a ‘host’ of grandchildren. Also, collards last for a long time if refrigerated.

The next step is to boil the stock and add the greens. Miss Freida recommends filling the pot to the top with the greens and then boiling for around 30 minutes, until tender. Remove the greens from the pot using a slotted spoon, place in a favorite serving bowl and call the family to supper.

The remaining stock is known as pot liquor and some enjoy drinking it. Miss Freida also suggests purchasing some Jiffy vegetarian cornbread mix, dropping it in the stock and reheating for some amazing collard cornbread dumplings.

“The secret to making your food taste good is to have good intentions. If you feel good when you’re doing it, the love flows into the food,” added Miss Freida. “A great thing about this recipe is that you can use it with kale, mustard greens or cabbage too. I believe it’s important to be creative in the kitchen.”

The recipe provided by Mrs. Lydia Heines is not as vegetarian-friendly, though it sounds equally delicious. “I don’t know why people think mine are so good except I do use a whole lot of bacon grease. I guess you could say bacon grease is the secret,” laughed Mrs. Lydia.

The process begins, of course, by making a pan of bacon. To create her stock, Mrs. Lydia starts by placing a ham hock into a pot of chicken stock, cut with a little bit of water. She then adds chopped Vidalia onion, then the bacon grease, to get everything stewing together.

Prior to adding the collards, Mrs. Lydia also incorporates a little Worcestershire and a little apple cider vinegar, along with about a half of a clove of chopped garlic. “I have it written down in my cookbook in Wilmington, but I’ve been doing it by memory up here,” said Mrs. Lydia, who was vacationing in the Boone area at the time.

A few of the other additions that Mrs. Lydia includes in her stock include Old Bay seasoning, salt and pepper, and a little sugar. “I’ve got some secret spice rubs up here that people make and give to me, so I put some of that in there too,” added Mrs. Lydia. “And like I said, a lot of bacon grease. When I say a lot, I mean at least a half a cup or more, depending on how much you’re cooking.”

She credited her deceased father-in-law, who owned the Miljo Drive-In on Oleander, for teaching her how to prepare the collards. “He was a home cook and taught me how to trim the collards and put them into the pot. He had nothing to do with the stock I make but he showed me how to trim the collards and cut them up properly,” noted Mrs. Lydia.

Similar to Miss Freida, Mrs. Lydia boils the collards for around 20 to 30 minutes until they are dark green in color. She then lets them sit to soak up all the flavor of the stock, and then once they have cooled, she removes them from the pot for another round of chopping, to ensure the collards are fork-ready. After the collards are removed from the pot, Mrs. Lydia takes the ham hock out, trims the fat and shreds the remaining portions of meat.

While the collards are out, she squeezes them and uses a paper towel to remove some of the remaining water. “A lot of people don’t want them to be swimming, but it really just depends on how you want to eat them,” added Mrs. Lydia.

The stock is placed to the side for use as a base for chicken and vegetable soup. The final step is to place the collards and ham hock pieces into a serving bowl. “Collards are good for you and they keep for a pretty long time. I like to make a big batch so I can freeze them and have them on hand,” said Mrs. Lydia.

Though including bacon grease may impact the health benefits somewhat, it is worth pointing out that collards include a range of beneficial vitamins and minerals. A few of these include vitamin A, vitamin K, vitamin B-6, vitamin C and calcium. They can also be a good source of iron, niacin, pantothenic acid, thiamin, choline and magnesium.

And, with the idea in mind that there is no such thing as a right or wrong answer in the kitchen, it’s worth noting that, in other areas, it is not uncommon to sauté collards in a stock of olive oil, garlic and onions. Some even like them raw in a salad or infused into a smoothie. An especially mouth-watering idea involves tossing collard leaves in olive oil, and then baking at 275 degrees for 30 minutes. After they are done, sprinkle with some curry, red pepper flakes, garlic powder, cumin and/or chili powder (or just one of these), and the result is a portable, healthy snack that is ready for any occasion. Bon appetite, y’all.
